Step 1

Give the tool an application and a hardware library

The application is a list of additions and multiplications (a data-flow graph). The library says how big and how slow each hardware unit is, and how many are available. PSO then searches for the number of adders and multipliers that best balances chip area against speed.

Step 2

Watch the swarm search

Every square is one possible design. The numbered dots are particles: each one tries a design, remembers its own best, and gets pulled towards the swarm's best. Press play, or drag the slider.

Design space

Two dots in one square means two particles are testing the same design, which is allowed. Big jumps are expected too: after moving, every particle is mutated on purpose (swapped or bumped by ±1–3) so the swarm doesn't get stuck. Dashed trail = mutation jump, solid trail = velocity move.

Convergence

The swarm's best design cost after every iteration, drawn as the replay advances. It can only go down. The search stops when it stays flat for 10 iterations in a row, or at the hard limit of 50.

Step 3

The best design

Scheduled data-flow graph

Rows are clock cycles (control steps), columns are the hardware units; “busy 4/10” means one unit is reused in 4 cycles. A cycle is as long as its slowest operation. Hover an operation to trace its inputs.

Register allocation table (RAT)

Same format as Table 1 of the paper: one row per register (named by colour), one column per control step, and the storage variable each register holds. Hover a variable to highlight where it is stored.

How the cost is computed

Design cost = t1·(A − Ac)/Amax + t2·(L − Lc)/Lmax (Eq. 5 of the paper). More negative is better.

Multiplexers & demultiplexers

Reusing a unit or register means it receives data from several places (needs a mux) or sends to several places (needs a demux).

Step 4

Area vs speed: the trade-off

More hardware units make the design faster but bigger. Designs on the dashed Pareto front can't be made smaller without becoming slower. The star is the design with the lowest cost. Click any point to inspect that design in step 3.

Background

How it works, in five ideas

1

Data-flow graph

The algorithm (e.g. a DCT) is written as operations: *,0,0,1 means “operation 1 multiplies two inputs”, +,1,2,5 means “operation 5 adds the results of operations 1 and 2”.

2

Scheduling

With a fixed number of adders and multipliers, operations are packed into clock cycles (list scheduling). An operation waits until its inputs are ready and a free unit exists. Each cycle is as long as its slowest operation.

3

Registers

Values are stored in registers between cycles. Two values that are never alive at the same time can share a register (left-edge register colouring), so the chip needs fewer of them.

4

Design cost

Area = units + registers. Latency = sum of cycle lengths. Cost = 0.5·(Area − Ac)/Amax + 0.5·(Latency − Lc)/Lmax. With the constraints Ac, Lc at their worst-case values the cost is always negative; more negative is better.

5

Particle swarm

Instead of trying every combination, a few particles start at the max, min and average configurations. Each iteration they move towards their own best and the swarm's best (inertia ω falls 0.9→0.1), mutate to keep exploring, and the search stops after 50 iterations or 10 without improvement.
